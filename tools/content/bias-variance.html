The trade-off

Every model's error on new data has two sources. Bias is the error from assumptions that are too simple: a straight line through a curve is wrong however much data you feed it. Variance is the error from being too sensitive to the particular sample: a wiggly curve that passes through every training point changes wildly when the sample changes.

Making a model more complex lowers bias and raises variance. Training error keeps falling, but error on unseen data falls and then climbs again. That climb is overfitting.

How to use it

  1. Judge on held-out data. Training error always flatters a complex model. Use a validation set or cross-validation.
  2. Read the gap. Poor on both train and test means high bias: add features or flexibility. Great on train, poor on test means high variance: simplify, regularise or get more data.
  3. Match complexity to data volume. The sweet spot moves right as you collect more examples.
  4. Start simple. A baseline you can explain tells you how much the extra complexity is actually buying.

Example

A team predicts weekly demand from 15 weeks of history. A 9th-degree curve hits every past week exactly and forecasts absurd swings. A straight trend line misses the seasonal bump. A low-degree curve with a seasonal term is slightly wrong everywhere and is the one that holds up next quarter.

Watch out for

  • Peeking at the test set. Tuning against it quietly turns it into training data and hides the variance you built in.
  • The classic U-shape isn't universal. Very large models trained on huge datasets can behave differently, so measure rather than assume.
  • The same shape appears in exact vs. approximate: more effort has diminishing returns.
