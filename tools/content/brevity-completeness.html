The trade-off

A longer message can answer more questions in advance: background, options, risks. But attention is limited. With every paragraph, fewer people read to the end, and the key point gets buried.

What a message achieves is roughly what it covers × how many people actually take it in. That peaks somewhere in the middle, and where depends on how much your audience already knows.

How to use it

  1. Lead with the bottom line. State the decision, ask or conclusion in the first sentence (BLUF: bottom line up front). Readers who stop early still get it.
  2. Write for a specific reader. What do they already know? Cut anything they don't need in order to act.
  3. Layer the detail. Short summary first, then supporting points, then links or appendices for those who want depth.
  4. Test it: could someone act correctly after reading only the first two lines?

Example

Before: a 900-word update describing the week's investigation, with the request to approve a budget increase in paragraph seven.

After: "Please approve +$4k for extra API capacity by Friday. Without it, the launch slips two weeks. Details below." Same facts, but the decision now happens the same day.

Watch out for

  • Too short creates follow-up questions, and the back-and-forth costs more than a longer first message would have.
  • Different channels, different lengths. A chat message, an email and a design document each have a different sweet spot.
  • How you say it matters as much as how much. See candour vs. diplomacy.
