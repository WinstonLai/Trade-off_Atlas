The trade-off

Scanning every row gives the exact answer, but costs time and money in proportion to the data. A sample, or an approximate algorithm (HyperLogLog for distinct counts, t-digest for percentiles), gets close for a fraction of the cost.

Statistical error falls with 1/√n. The first few thousand rows buy most of the precision. After that, each extra decimal place costs far more compute than the one before.

How to use it

  1. Ask what precision the decision needs. If you'd act the same way whether the answer is 39% or 41%, ±1 point is plenty.
  2. Separate exploring from reporting. Use samples and approximations while exploring, and exact numbers for finance, billing and compliance.
  3. Always show the error bar. An approximate number with an honest ± is more useful than a "precise" one nobody trusts.
  4. Watch rare segments. Small groups need bigger samples (or stratified sampling) to be estimated well.

Example

An analyst wants to check whether a new checkout flow changed conversion. A 1% sample of a billion events answers in seconds with well under ±0.1 points of error, plenty to see a 2-point lift. The full scan would take 40 minutes on an expensive cluster and move the answer by a hundredth of a percent.

Watch out for

  • Sampling error isn't the only error. A biased sample (for example, only weekday data) is precisely wrong.
  • Money must add up. Invoices, balances and audits need exact sums.
  • Approximate answers also come faster, which links this trade-off to batch vs. streaming.
