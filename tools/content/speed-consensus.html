The trade-off

Deciding alone is fast, but people who weren't consulted may resist, and you miss what they knew. Seeking full consensus builds commitment, but every extra voice adds meetings, objections and waiting. Buy-in levels off while time keeps climbing.

The right balance depends on reversibility. A two-way door (a pricing test, a tool trial) can be undone cheaply, so decide fast and adjust. A one-way door (a reorganization, a platform migration, a public commitment) is expensive to reverse, so wider input is worth the wait.

How to use it

  1. Classify the decision. How much would it cost to undo in three months?
  2. Name the decider. Make clear who decides and who is consulted or informed (RACI, RAPID). Consulting isn't voting.
  3. Set a deadline for input. "Comments by Thursday, decision Friday" stops consultation from running on forever.
  4. Use "disagree and commit". Once the decision is made, people who argued against it commit to making it work.

Example

A product lead picks the colour of a new onboarding banner after a quick chat with design. It's a reversible choice, settled in an hour. Retiring a product line used by key customers gets a two-week consultation with sales, support and finance, because an abrupt reversal would damage trust.

Watch out for

  • Consensus can hide responsibility. When everyone decides, nobody owns the outcome.
  • Speed can look like arrogance. Explain the reasoning even when you didn't consult widely.
  • Group size raises coordination cost here too, just like Brooks's law.
