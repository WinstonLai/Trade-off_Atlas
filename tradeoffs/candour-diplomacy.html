Candour vs. diplomacy

Direct feedback is clear but can bruise. Soft feedback protects feelings but often doesn't land. The way out is to be direct and visibly caring at the same time.

Directnessvs.Diplomacy

The trade-off

When giving feedback, it can feel like there's one dial: be honest and risk the relationship, or be kind and risk the message getting lost. Kim Scott's Radical Candor framework splits it into two separate axes: how directly you challenge, and how much you show you care personally.

  • Radical candour (high both): clear, and clearly on their side.
  • Obnoxious aggression (direct, no care): clear, but damages trust.
  • Ruinous empathy (care, not direct): pleasant, but the problem persists.
  • Manipulative insincerity (neither): vague, often political.

How to use it

  1. Check the relationship first. Directness lands better when the person already knows you're on their side. Build that trust before you need it.
  2. Be specific about the behaviour, not the person. "Three figures in section 2 were wrong" rather than "you're careless".
  3. Say why it matters, and offer help. Connect the feedback to their goals and the team's.
  4. Give it soon and in private (praise can be public). Small, timely feedback is easier to hear than a stored-up list.

Example

A manager notices a team member talks over colleagues in meetings. Ruinous empathy would stay silent. Aggression would call it out in the meeting. Candour sounds like: "In today's review you cut Priya off twice, and I think we lost her idea. I want your influence to grow here, and this is getting in the way. Can we talk about it?"

Watch out for

  • Cultures differ. What sounds direct in one culture can sound rude in another. Adjust the wording, not the honesty.
  • "Just being honest" isn't a licence to be harsh. Care has to be shown, not assumed.
  • Keep it brief, too. See brevity vs. completeness.
