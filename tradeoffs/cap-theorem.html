Software development

CAP theorem

When the network between copies of your data breaks, you must choose: refuse requests to stay correct, or keep answering and risk stale data.

Consistencyvs.Availability

The trade-off

A distributed system keeps copies of data on several machines. The CAP theorem says that during a network partition (P), when the machines can't talk to each other, you can't have both:

  • Consistency (C): every read sees the latest write, or gets an error.
  • Availability (A): every request gets a non-error response, even if it may be out of date.

Partitions will happen in any real network, so the practical question is what your system does when one occurs.

How to use it

  1. Ask what a wrong answer costs. A double-spent bank balance or an oversold seat is expensive, so lean CP. A slightly stale like count or product review is harmless, so lean AP.
  2. Decide per feature, not per system. Checkout can be CP while the product catalogue is AP.
  3. Plan the reconciliation. If you choose AP, decide how conflicting writes merge when the partition heals (last write wins, merge, or ask the user).
  4. Remember the everyday version. Even with no partition, stronger consistency usually costs latency (PACELC).

Example

An e-commerce site stores shopping carts in an AP store: a cart is always writable, and duplicate items are merged later. Inventory reservations and payments go through a CP database that refuses the order rather than selling stock it can't confirm.

Watch out for

  • "Pick two of three" is misleading. You can't opt out of partitions. The choice only matters while one is happening.
  • It's a spectrum. Many databases let you tune consistency per query (quorum reads, read-your-writes).
  • The same tension shows up in data modelling. See normalization vs. denormalization.
