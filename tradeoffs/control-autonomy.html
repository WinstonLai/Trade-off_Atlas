Project management

Control vs. autonomy (delegation)

Reviewing more of the work catches more mistakes but makes everything wait for you. The right amount of oversight depends on how experienced the people are and how costly the mistakes are.

Controlvs.Autonomy

The trade-off

Every decision that needs approval waits for the approver, and the approver's time is limited. Oversight buys fewer mistakes reaching customers, and it buys them with delay, manager time and less ownership for the people doing the work. The two costs move in opposite directions, so total cost is lowest somewhere in between.

Where that point sits depends on the team. Experienced people make few mistakes, so each extra review catches little and costs the same. New people make more, so reviews pay for themselves. Jurgen Appelo's seven levels of delegation (tell, sell, consult, agree, advise, inquire, delegate) make the dial concrete. The point is that the level is chosen per kind of decision, not once per person, and it can be moved as trust builds.

How to use it

  1. Split decisions by cost of error. Reserve approval for the expensive, hard-to-reverse ones and let the rest go through (reversibility is the guide).
  2. Match the level to experience. Start closer to the manager's side for new people and tasks, and move towards delegation as the track record builds.
  3. Agree the level out loud. People resent surprise reviews far more than expected ones. Say which decisions are theirs, which need a consult and which need sign-off.
  4. Replace gates with visibility. Cheap ways to see the work (demos, dashboards, short written updates) can catch problems without making anyone wait.

Example

A manager with six reports approves every customer refund. It takes about a day each, the queue grows, and the senior agents are frustrated. Refunds under a threshold are delegated to the experienced agents, who make mistakes on about 8% of them; new hires keep needing sign-off for the first two months. Review time falls from many hours a week to a handful, and refund time falls from a day to minutes for most cases.

Watch out for

  • Control tends to feed itself. When a manager keeps changing what is wanted or reviewing everything, quality drops, and that looks like proof that more review is needed.
  • The manager is a bottleneck. Past a point, the queue for approval is the biggest cost of all (see utilization vs. flow).
  • The curves here are a model. Real mistake rates are not known in advance, and the cost of delay and of an error vary enormously between decisions.
