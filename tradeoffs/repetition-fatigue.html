Repetition vs. fatigue

A message said once is easily missed. Said too often, in the same words, it gets tuned out. The best number of repeats depends on how hard the message is to take in and how much you vary it.

Repetitionvs.Fatigue

The trade-off

People don't take in a message the first time they hear it. They're busy, the message competes with others, and understanding builds with each exposure. That is the case for saying it again. But each repeat also adds a little boredom and irritation, and after a while the audience stops attending at all. That is the case for stopping.

Advertising researchers describe this as a two-factor view: a learning effect that rises with exposure and a tedium effect that rises as well, usually later. Together they give a hump: impact climbs, peaks and falls. Two things move the peak. Complex messages need more exposures to land. And varied delivery (a new example, channel or messenger each time) delays the tedium, so you can repeat more before people tune out.

How to use it

  1. Expect to repeat. For anything that matters (a change, a deadline, a policy) plan several touches, not one announcement.
  2. Vary the form, not the content. Same point, different wording: a story one time, a number the next, a diagram after that.
  3. Use different channels and voices. Hearing it from the CEO, the team lead and a short written note is repetition people experience as reinforcement.
  4. Check that it landed, then stop. A quick question or a metric beats guessing, and once people can repeat the message back, more repetition only costs attention.

Example

A security team sends the same "don't click suspicious links" email every week. Click rates fall for the first month, then flatten, and open rates sink. They switch to a monthly cycle: one short phishing quiz, one real incident story, one reminder at the login screen. Open rates recover and click rates keep falling, with fewer messages in total.

Watch out for

  • The numbers are a model. Published estimates of the "right" frequency vary widely between studies, media and audiences. Treat any fixed number of repeats with suspicion and measure your own.
  • Irritation is not always wear-out. Sometimes people are annoyed by being over-targeted, not by the message itself, so fixing reach can help more than rewriting (reach vs. relevance).
  • Repetition can't rescue an unclear message. If the first version is too long or full of jargon, saying it again just repeats the problem (brevity vs. completeness, precision vs. plain language).
