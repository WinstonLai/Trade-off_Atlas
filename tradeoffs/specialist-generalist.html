Project management

Specialist vs. generalist

Specialists do their kind of work faster and better. Generalists can flex to wherever the queue is. The right mix depends on how predictable the work is.

Depthvs.Flexibility

The trade-off

Specialists are faster and produce better work in their area, but work only they can do queues up behind them. If one of them is away, that work stops (a low bus factor). Generalists are slower per task, but can pick up whatever is waiting, so work flows even when demand shifts.

If the mix of work is predictable and matches your staffing, specialists win on throughput. As the mix gets uneven or uncertain, the cost of idle specialists and blocked work grows, and flexibility starts to win.

How to use it

  1. Look at where work waits. A permanent queue in front of one skill is the signal that you're short of that skill or depend on one person.
  2. Grow "T-shaped" people: one deep area, plus enough breadth in the neighbouring ones to unblock the team.
  3. Pair on the risky skills so no critical area has a single owner.
  4. Keep deep experts for the hard problems and avoid spending them on routine work others can do.

Example

A team has one database specialist. In a month with few schema changes, they are idle and everything flows. In the month of a migration, every story waits for their review, and a week of illness stops the release. Teaching two developers the basics means those people take 30% longer on a database task, but nothing waits on a single person any more.

Watch out for

  • Jack of all trades. If nobody goes deep, the hard problems never get solved well.
  • Sharing everyone across everything pushes utilisation up and raises waiting time for the same reason.
  • More generalists means more overlap and communication. Compare Brooks's law.
