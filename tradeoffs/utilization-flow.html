Project management

Utilization vs. flow

A team that is 100% busy cannot respond. As utilization climbs past roughly 80%, waiting time doesn't rise gently. It explodes.

Utilizationvs.Responsiveness

The trade-off

Managers often aim for full utilization, so that nobody is idle. But work doesn't arrive evenly. When a team has no slack, every surprise joins a queue. Queueing theory (Kingman's formula) shows that waiting time grows roughly with u / (1 − u): going from 50% to 90% busy doesn't double the wait, it multiplies it by nine.

The more variable the work (unclear sizes, interruptions, urgent requests), the earlier the curve bends. Slack isn't waste. It's what lets the system flow.

How to use it

  1. Decide which you're optimizing: busy people or fast delivery. Measure lead time (request to done), not only how busy people are.
  2. Plan for 70–80% capacity. Leave room for support, bugs and the unexpected.
  3. Limit work in progress. Finishing before starting shrinks the queue directly.
  4. Reduce variability: split large tasks, standardize recurring ones, and protect the team from interruptions.

Example

A data team runs at "full capacity" and everyone's calendar is packed. A simple dashboard request takes six weeks, not because it's hard but because it waits behind everything else. Reserving 20% of each sprint for incoming requests cuts the typical turnaround to days, even though the planned roadmap delivers slightly less.

Watch out for

  • Busy ≠ productive. High utilization can hide very slow delivery.
  • The 80% line is a rule of thumb. The real knee depends on how variable your work is.
  • It applies to machines too: servers, databases and pipelines near 100% CPU show the same latency spike. See batch vs. streaming.
