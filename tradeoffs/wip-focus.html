Parallel work vs. focus (WIP limits)

Starting more things at once feels productive, but every switch between them costs time and delays all of them. Limiting work in progress finishes more, sooner.

Work in parallelvs.Focus

The trade-off

Running several tasks at once keeps people from being blocked and makes everyone look busy. But each switch carries a context-switching cost: re-reading, remembering where you were, regaining momentum. Gerald Weinberg's rule of thumb is that each additional task takes around 20% of the day, so three tasks leave about 60% for real work.

There's a second effect. If all the tasks progress together, they all finish late. Little's law says average cycle time equals work in progress divided by throughput, so for a given throughput, more things in flight means a longer wait for each of them. A WIP limit caps the number of items in progress so that people finish before they start.

How to use it

  1. Make work visible on a board and count what's in progress per person or team.
  2. Set a limit a little below today's typical load (one or two items per person is a common start), and stop starting when you hit it.
  3. When blocked, fix the blocker first. Pulling in a new task is the habit the limit is meant to break.
  4. Watch cycle time, not busyness. If a limit is working, items leave the board faster.

Example

An analyst has five reports in flight because each is waiting on someone. All five are "almost done" for a month. After agreeing a limit of two, the analyst chases the blocked ones rather than starting new ones. The first report ships in a week, the others follow at a steady pace, and stakeholders see results instead of status updates.

Watch out for

  • Zero parallelism isn't the goal. Real waits (a review, a vendor) are worth filling with other work. The cost grows with the number of active tasks, not with ever having a second one.
  • The 20% is an approximation. Switching between similar tasks in the same codebase costs less than switching between a design review and a customer call.
  • Related: utilization vs. flow explains why a fully booked team has long queues; this one explains why a person with many tasks has slow ones. Specialists can reduce switching, but create hand-offs.
